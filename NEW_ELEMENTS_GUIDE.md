# New Workspace Elements: A Plain-Language Guide

**Who this is for:** anyone who has to understand, explain or approve the new elements added to the project workspace, with no technical background needed.

**What you will find:** for every element, the same short story:

- **In plain words:** what it is, as you would describe it to a colleague.
- **The problem it solves:** what goes wrong without it.
- **When you would use it:** a realistic everyday scenario.
- **How it is used:** the steps, in order.
- **What it gives you:** the benefit, in business terms.
- **Good to know:** limits and things to watch for.

The scenarios are examples written to make the idea easy to picture. The behaviour described for each element is how it actually works.

---

## Part A. The big picture (read this first)

### What is the workspace?

The workspace is a shared digital table, called the **canvas**, where everyone on a project works in one place: the vendor doing the work, the Project Manager (PM) running it, the client paying for it, and any CAS members who support the PM.

Instead of project information being scattered across WhatsApp groups, e-mails, photos on phones and paper files, it sits on one canvas, in the right task, where the right people can see it.

### What is an "element"?

An element is a **ready-made building block** you drop onto the canvas. Each one is designed for a specific job: a signature box, a defect list, a price calculator, a permit.

Think of elements as **printed forms that fill themselves in and do their own sums**. Nobody has to design a form or build a spreadsheet. You pick the block, fill it in, and everyone sees it.

### Why add new ones?

Projects in different trades keep making the same costly mistakes:

| The mistake | The cost | What the new elements do about it |
|---|---|---|
| A job is priced wrongly | Lost profit | Calculators work out labour, tax and margin correctly |
| Feedback on a design is vague | Reprints and delays | Numbered pins and approved versions make feedback exact |
| "I never approved that" | Disputes and free rework | Every approval is stamped with name, date and time |
| A licence or product expires unnoticed | Fines, wasted stock | Trackers warn before the date arrives |
| A defect is "fixed" but nobody checks | Failed handover | Each item must be checked by a named person |
| A risky job starts without a safety check | Accidents | A permit must be approved before work begins |
| Measurements are noted once and lost | Wrong quantities, wrong bills | Sheets keep and total the measurements |

### The six groups

| Group | Who it is mainly for | Count |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Cost calculators | Anyone who quotes or plans work | 6 |
| 2. Everyday basics | Every project, every trade | 10 |
| 3. Printing and signage | Boards, banners, print and packaging vendors | 5 |
| 4. Food and FMCG | Food makers and packaged-goods suppliers | 5 |
| 5. Interiors and furniture | Interior designers, fit-out and furniture makers | 5 |
| 6. Construction | Contractors and site teams | 6 |

**Total: 37 new elements.**

### Three ideas that appear again and again

Once you understand these, most of the elements make sense on their own.

1. **Nothing is guessed.** If a number cannot be worked out, the element says so and leaves it out of the total. It never invents a figure and hides the problem.
2. **Numbers never get re-used.** Pin 3, snag 5 and version 2 keep their numbers even when others are deleted. "See snag 5" always means the same snag.
3. **Approvals leave a trace.** When someone approves something, their name and the time are stamped on it where everyone can see. After approval, the element is locked until someone deliberately reopens it, and the earlier decision stays on record.

## Part B. Who approves what (the approval rules)

Five elements need a sign-off: the **Permit to Work**, **Inspection Checklist**, **Artwork Approval Card**, **Moodboard** and **Batch Record**. They all follow the same rules, so it is explained once here.

### In plain words

The person who creates the element chooses **who should approve it**. The system only offers people they are allowed to ask, and it makes sure the request goes up the chain in the right order.

### Who can ask whom

| Who sends it | They can ask | What happens next |
|---|---|---|
| **Vendor** | The **PM** or a **CAS member**. Never the client directly. | The PM forwards it to the **client**, and it closes when the client approves. If a CAS member was asked first: CAS member, then PM, then client. |
| **CAS member** | The **PM** | PM, then client |
| **Client** | The **PM** | The PM forwards it to a **vendor**, and it closes when the vendor approves. |
| **PM** | Anyone: vendors, the client, CAS members | That person approves and it closes. |

### Why it is built this way

A vendor should not be able to go over the PM's head to the client with something the PM has not seen. The PM stays in control of what reaches the client, and the client only sees what the PM is willing to put in front of them.

### Other rules worth knowing

- **No client on the project?** The PM's approval closes it. The Batch Record is an internal quality step, so it closes with the PM or a CAS member and is never sent to the client.
- **Choosing who:** if there is only one person to ask, they are chosen for you. If there are several, you pick from a list.
- **Sent back:** anyone in the chain can send it back **with a reason**. It returns to the person who sent it, who fixes it and sends it again.
- **Everyone sees everything:** the element and every stamp on it (who approved, and when) is visible to all members on the canvas.
- **Locked while with an approver, and once approved.** To change it, reopen it. The earlier decision is kept.
- **Enforced by the system, not only by the screen.** A vendor cannot ask the client directly, and an approval cannot be forged or edited afterwards.

### A worked example

> A vendor finishes a Permit to Work for welding on the third floor. They choose the PM as approver. The PM checks it and approves. Because the project has a client, it now goes to the client, who approves too. The permit shows two stamps: "PM, 9:10 am" and "Client, 9:40 am". Welding can start. Nobody can later claim the permit was never approved, and nobody can quietly edit it, because it is locked.

## 1. Cost calculators (6 elements)

**The idea in one line:** quote and plan work with the right numbers, not guesses.

You already had calculators for materials such as cement, bricks and paint. These six cover **labour, pricing and everyday conversions**. Every rate they start with is an example figure. **Change it to your own rates** before relying on the answer.

### 1.1 Tiling Labour Calculator

- **In plain words:** tells you how many days a tiling job will take and what the labour will cost.
- **The problem it solves:** tiling quotes are often a rough guess per square foot. Quote too low and you lose money on labour. Quote too high and you lose the job.
- **When you would use it:** a client wants 1,200 sq ft of flooring tiled. Before sending the quote, you want to know how many days the crew will need and what the labour costs.
- **How it is used:**
  1. Enter the area to be tiled.
  2. Enter how much one mason lays in a day.
  3. Enter how many masons and helpers you have, and their daily wages.
  4. Add skirting if it is part of the job.
- **What it gives you:** the number of days, the labour cost, and the cost per square metre. That last number is the one you can compare against other quotes.
- **Good to know:** it covers labour, not the tiles themselves.

### 1.2 False Ceiling Calculator

- **In plain words:** works out the materials and the cost of a false ceiling.
- **The problem it solves:** ceilings involve many small items (boards, channels, screws, edges). Missing any of them means a second trip to the supplier or an under-priced quote.
- **When you would use it:** an office needs a gypsum ceiling in a 400 sq ft room with a cove edge. You want the full material list and the price before visiting the supplier.
- **How it is used:**
  1. Choose the type: gypsum board, grid tiles or PVC panels.
  2. Enter the area and the wastage allowance.
  3. Add any cove or drop edges.
- **What it gives you:** how many boards or tiles, how much channel, how many screws, and the total cost.
- **Good to know:** wastage is your own judgement. A tight room with many cuts needs a bigger allowance.

### 1.3 Plumbing Pipe Calculator

- **In plain words:** works out the pipes, fittings and cost for a plumbing run.
- **The problem it solves:** ordering "about enough" pipe leads to either leftover stock you paid for or a delay while you reorder.
- **When you would use it:** a bathroom block needs 60 metres of pipe run. You want the number of pipe lengths and fittings before placing the order.
- **How it is used:**
  1. Enter the total length of the run.
  2. Choose the pipe size and material.
- **What it gives you:** the number of pipe lengths, how many fittings (elbows, tees) you will need, and the cost.

### 1.4 Unit Converter

- **In plain words:** converts between the units people actually use on site.
- **The problem it solves:** drawings are in metres, the supplier quotes in feet, the land record is in cents or acres, and the client thinks in square feet. A wrong conversion becomes a wrong order.
- **When you would use it:** the drawing says 45 sq m but the tile supplier sells by square foot. You want the exact figure immediately.
- **How it is used:** type a number, pick the unit you have, and read the answer.
- **What it gives you:** the value in the unit you asked for, and also in every other unit of that kind. It covers sq ft and sq m, cu ft and cu m, acre, cent, guntha, feet, metres, quintal, tonne and more.
- **Good to know:** it removes a very common, very expensive, very avoidable error.

### 1.5 Labour Productivity Calculator

- **In plain words:** shows how long a job will take and what the labour will cost, based on the kind of work.
- **The problem it solves:** you are not sure whether the labour allowance in a quote is realistic.
- **When you would use it:** a plastering job of 2,000 sq ft is quoted with a three-day crew. Is three days believable? This tells you.
- **How it is used:**
  1. Pick the kind of work (brickwork, plastering, painting and so on).
  2. Enter the quantity.
  3. Enter the crew size.
- **What it gives you:** the number of days, the total cost, and the **cost per unit of work**.
- **Good to know:** the productivity figures are starting points. Adjust them to what your crews actually achieve.

### 1.6 GST & Margin Calculator

- **In plain words:** prices a job from your cost, or splits an amount that already includes GST.
- **The problem it solves:** two very common money mistakes. First, confusing **markup** with **margin**, which leads to under-pricing. Second, getting the tax wrong on a quote.
- **When you would use it:**
  - You know the job costs you 80,000 and you want a 25% profit margin. What price do you quote?
  - A supplier gives a lump sum of 1,18,000 "including GST". What is the real price and the tax?
- **How it is used:** choose one of three modes:
  1. **Add a markup** to the cost.
  2. **Aim for a target profit margin.**
  3. **Take apart a GST-inclusive amount.**
- **What it gives you:** profit, CGST and SGST (or IGST), and the invoice total.
- **Good to know:** a 25% markup and a 25% margin are *not* the same price. A 25% margin needs a higher selling price than a 25% markup. This calculator shows the difference so you do not under-price by mistake.

## 2. Everyday basics (10 elements)

**The idea in one line:** general-purpose blocks that are useful on any project, in any trade.

### 2.1 Signature Pad

- **In plain words:** a box where a person signs with a mouse, finger or stylus.
- **The problem it solves:** sign-off normally means printing a paper, getting it signed, scanning it and chasing the person. Or worse, a verbal "okay" that is later denied.
- **When you would use it:** at handover, the client signs to confirm the work is received. You want that signature stored next to the work.
- **How it is used:**
  1. The signer types their name and role.
  2. They sign in the box.
  3. If the signature is not good, clear it and sign again.
- **What it gives you:** the signature, the signer's name and role, and the date and time, all kept together. A clear record of who agreed, and when.
- **Good to know:** it is a clear working record, not a legal e-signature.

### 2.2 Photo Grid

- **In plain words:** up to six photos, each with a caption, in one tidy block.
- **The problem it solves:** site photos end up on phones and in chat groups, and nobody can find them when they are needed.
- **When you would use it:** at the end of each week you want to show the client how the work stands: foundation, columns, wiring, delivery of materials.
- **How it is used:** add the photos and write a short caption under each.
- **What it gives you:** photos that live next to the work they belong to, in the right task.
- **Good to know:** photos are saved inside a subtask, so open one before uploading.

### 2.3 Before / After

- **In plain words:** two photos side by side, labelled Before and After.
- **The problem it solves:** it is hard to show how much has changed, and hard to prove a repair was needed in the first place.
- **When you would use it:** a damaged wall was repaired. You show the cracked wall and the finished wall together, so the client sees the value of the work.
- **How it is used:** add the Before photo and the After photo.
- **What it gives you:** simple, convincing proof of progress or repair.

### 2.4 Location Pin

- **In plain words:** a map with an address and coordinates, with an "Open in Maps" link.
- **The problem it solves:** "Where is the site?" is one of the most common messages on any project, and wrong directions waste hours.
- **When you would use it:** a delivery driver needs to find a site on a new road. You place the pin once, and anyone can use it.
- **How it is used:**
  1. Enter the address, or tap "Use my location" to fill the coordinates from your phone.
  2. Others tap "Open in Maps" to get directions.
- **What it gives you:** one correct location, shared with everyone, with directions one tap away.

### 2.5 Rating

- **In plain words:** a row of stars (3, 5 or 10) that you click to set.
- **The problem it solves:** opinions on quality are scattered in conversations and never summarised.
- **When you would use it:** rating a supplier's delivery, the quality of a finish, or the client's satisfaction at the end of a stage.
- **How it is used:** click the star that matches your score.
- **What it gives you:** a quick, visible score that everyone can read at a glance.

### 2.6 Date Range

- **In plain words:** a start date and an end date, with the number of days between them.
- **The problem it solves:** periods of work, bookings and contract terms are written as text, and people misread them.
- **When you would use it:** a hired scaffold is on site from the 3rd to the 18th. You want everyone to see that clearly and to know when it is due to go.
- **How it is used:** pick the start date and the end date.
- **What it gives you:** the number of days, and a plain status: "Starts in 5 days", "3 days left", "Last day" or "Ended". It warns you if the end date is before the start date.

### 2.7 Number with Unit

- **In plain words:** one important number shown large, with its unit.
- **The problem it solves:** key figures such as total area or capacity get buried inside paragraphs of text.
- **When you would use it:** the total built-up area of a project is 1,20,000 m². You want it to be the first thing anyone sees.
- **How it is used:** type the number and choose the unit.
- **What it gives you:** a headline figure that cannot be missed.

### 2.8 QR Code

- **In plain words:** a scannable square code that holds a link or a short piece of text.
- **The problem it solves:** printed papers and physical labels are cut off from the digital information about them.
- **When you would use it:** you print a drawing and want anyone on site to be able to scan it and open the latest version. Or you stick a code on equipment that links to its manual.
- **How it is used:** enter the link or text, and the code appears. Print it or show it.
- **What it gives you:** a bridge between paper and the workspace. Anyone can scan it with a phone to open the item.

### 2.9 Barcode

- **In plain words:** a standard barcode for a reference number (up to 40 characters).
- **The problem it solves:** matching delivered goods to paperwork by reading numbers aloud is slow and error-prone.
- **When you would use it:** you tag boxes and files with a reference so that checking stock in and out is a scan, not a search.
- **How it is used:** enter the reference number and the barcode is generated.
- **What it gives you:** faster check-in of stock and fewer mismatches between goods and documents.

### 2.10 Callout

- **In plain words:** a highlighted note in one of four styles: Info, Warning, Success or Critical.
- **The problem it solves:** the one message that matters is lost among ordinary text.
- **When you would use it:** "Do not load the slab for 48 hours" must be seen by everyone who opens the canvas. A Critical callout makes sure it is.
- **How it is used:** write the message and choose the style that fits its importance.
- **What it gives you:** important messages that stand out and are announced clearly to everyone who opens the canvas.

## 3. Printing and signage (5 elements)

**The idea in one line:** get a clear, recorded "yes" before anything is printed or built.

This is where jobs most often go wrong. Feedback is unclear, changes are made without approval, and the reprint is paid for by the vendor. These five elements are built to prevent exactly that.

### 3.1 Proof Markup

- **In plain words:** the artwork with numbered pins dropped on it. Each pin carries a comment and a status: Open, Fixed or Approved.
- **The problem it solves:** feedback such as "make the logo a bit bigger and fix the thing at the bottom" is vague, and the designer has to guess.
- **When you would use it:** a client reviews a shop-front banner design. They drop a pin on the logo ("too small") and another on the phone number ("wrong digit"). The designer fixes each one and marks it Fixed.
- **How it is used:**
  1. Add the artwork.
  2. Click where the issue is to drop a pin, and write the comment.
  3. Drag a pin if it is in the wrong place.
  4. As each issue is dealt with, change its status to Fixed, then Approved.
- **What it gives you:**
  - Exact feedback: "Pin 3: logo too small" is unmistakable.
  - Pin numbers **never change**, even if another pin is deleted, so "see pin 3" always means the same thing.
  - A summary such as "2 open · 1 fixed · 4 approved" tells you how close the proof is to done.

### 3.2 Artwork Approval Card

- **In plain words:** the artwork, its version number (v1, v2, v3) and the decision on it.
- **The problem it solves:** an approval given for version 1 gets mistaken for approval of version 3, which has changed. The wrong artwork is printed and nobody agrees who is responsible.
- **When you would use it:** a packaging design goes through three rounds of change. The client approves v3. If a dispute comes up later, the card shows exactly what was approved, and when.
- **How it is used:**
  1. Add the artwork and choose who approves (see Part B).
  2. The approver approves or sends it back with a reason.
  3. When the artwork changes, add a new version.
- **What it gives you:**
  - **A new version always starts as "not sent".** An old approval can never be mistaken for approval of changed artwork.
  - Earlier versions stay in a list, so there is a history if a dispute arises.
  - Every approval is stamped with name and time.

### 3.3 Signage Elevation

- **In plain words:** a drawing of a sign board, to scale, showing width, height and the height at which it is mounted.
- **The problem it solves:** the client imagines one thing, the fabricator builds another, and the installer finds the wall is the wrong size.
- **When you would use it:** you are making a 10 ft by 3 ft board to be fixed 8 ft above the pavement. You want the client and the installation team to see the same drawing.
- **How it is used:** type the width, height and mounting height. The drawing follows. List the material, frame, lighting and finish underneath.
- **What it gives you:** a scaled drawing with the dimensions marked and the board's area shown, plus the specification, all in one place.

### 3.4 Colour Palette

- **In plain words:** a set of colour swatches, each with a name and its HEX, CMYK and Pantone values.
- **The problem it solves:** a brand colour looks right on screen and wrong on the printed board. Different people use slightly different versions of "the red".
- **When you would use it:** the client's brand uses a specific blue. You record its values once so the designer, the printer and the signage maker all use the same one.
- **How it is used:**
  1. Add a swatch and enter its name and values.
  2. Tick "Matched" once the printed result has been checked against it.
- **What it gives you:** one reference for brand colours, with a count such as "3 of 5 matched". A value that cannot be read is outlined in red, so typing errors are caught.

### 3.5 Material Swatch Board

- **In plain words:** cards for materials (vinyl, ACP, acrylic, paper and so on), each with a photo, finish, thickness and supplier.
- **The problem it solves:** material options are explained over many messages and the client's choice is never clearly recorded.
- **When you would use it:** before fabrication, you show the client three board materials side by side so they can choose.
- **How it is used:** add a card for each material option with its details and photo.
- **What it gives you:** material choices agreed from a single page, instead of scattered samples and messages.

## 4. Food and FMCG (5 elements)

**The idea in one line:** keep the records that prove your product is safe and your licences are valid.

In food and packaged goods, records are not paperwork for its own sake. They are what protects you when something goes wrong, and what an inspector asks to see.

> **Important:** these are tidy working records. They are not certified systems and do not replace any audit, statutory format or standard that your regulator or contract requires.

### 4.1 Batch Record

- **In plain words:** the record of one production batch: the product, batch number, date, ingredients with their lot numbers, planned and actual output, and the quality check.
- **The problem it solves:** if a problem is found in a product, you need to know exactly what went into that batch. Without a record, you cannot answer.
- **When you would use it:** a batch of 500 kg of sauce is made today. You record which lot of tomato paste and which lot of spices went in, how much was produced, and whether it passed the quality check.
- **How it is used:**
  1. Enter the product, batch number and date.
  2. Add each ingredient with its lot number.
  3. Enter the planned and actual output.
  4. Record the quality result and send it for approval (see Part B).
- **What it gives you:**
  - The **yield**: green when on target, amber when slightly under, red when well under.
  - An approval step with the PM or a CAS member. This is an internal quality step, so it closes there and is not sent to the client. A failed batch needs a reason.
  - **Once approved, the record is locked.** It can be reopened for a correction, and the earlier decision is kept on file, so an approved record cannot be quietly changed.

### 4.2 Expiry & Shelf-Life Tracker

- **In plain words:** a list of items with the date they were made and the date they expire.
- **The problem it solves:** stock is thrown away because nobody noticed the date, or worse, sold after expiry.
- **When you would use it:** a store room holds twenty items with different expiry dates. You want to know what to use or sell first.
- **How it is used:** add each item with its made-on and expiry dates.
- **What it gives you:** items sorted with the soonest expiry first, each marked green, amber or red with plain words ("15 days left", "Expired 2 days ago"), and a bar showing how much of its shelf life is used.
- **Good to know:** the warning period (for example, "amber 30 days before expiry") can be set to suit your business.

### 4.3 Temperature Log

- **In plain words:** a log of readings for a cold room, freezer or storage area, against the allowed range.
- **The problem it solves:** you need to prove that food was kept at a safe temperature, and a handwritten sheet is easy to lose or fill in afterwards.
- **When you would use it:** a cold room must stay between 2 and 6 degrees. Staff record the reading each shift.
- **How it is used:**
  1. Set the allowed range.
  2. Add each reading as it is taken.
- **What it gives you:**
  - Each reading marked **In range**, **Too cold** or **Too warm**.
  - A small chart showing the trend with the safe band shaded.
  - A count of how many readings went out of range.
  - It works for freezers too (negative temperatures).
  - A full log asks you to start a new one rather than quietly deleting old readings.

### 4.4 FSSAI / Licence Tracker

- **In plain words:** a list of licences and registrations with their number, the issuing authority and the expiry date.
- **The problem it solves:** a licence lapses unnoticed, and production or sale becomes illegal until it is renewed.
- **When you would use it:** a business holds a food licence, a fire certificate and a pollution consent, each expiring on a different date.
- **How it is used:** add each licence with its number, authority and expiry date.
- **What it gives you:** each licence shows "Valid", "Renew in 20 days" or "Expired 39 days ago", with a summary at the top so the one that needs attention is obvious.

### 4.5 Recall Trace Sheet

- **In plain words:** for one batch, the raw materials used and everyone the product was sent to.
- **The problem it solves:** if a product must be pulled back, speed and accuracy matter. Without a trace, you either recall too much or miss customers.
- **When you would use it:** a supplier reports a contaminated ingredient. You need to know which batch used it and which customers received that batch.
- **How it is used:**
  1. Enter the batch and the raw materials used.
  2. Add each customer it was dispatched to, with the quantity.
- **What it gives you:**
  - A total of how much went out against how much was made, and the number of customers.
  - A warning if more was dispatched than was produced.
  - **Copy trace** puts a ready-to-send summary on your clipboard for a regulator, a customer or a recall team.

## 5. Interiors and furniture (5 elements)

**The idea in one line:** keep many rooms, many finishes and many decisions in step.

An interior project has dozens of small decisions spread across many rooms. Mistakes usually appear on site, when they are expensive to fix. These elements put the decisions in one place.

### 5.1 Room-wise Scope Sheet

- **In plain words:** each room with the work items inside it, their finish and their status (To do, In progress, Done, On hold). Each room folds open and closed.
- **The problem it solves:** the client asks "what's the status?" and nobody can answer without walking the site or making calls.
- **When you would use it:** a four-bedroom apartment fit-out. The client wants to see, room by room, what is finished and what is pending.
- **How it is used:**
  1. Add each room.
  2. Add the work items in the room and their finish.
  3. Update each item's status as work progresses.
- **What it gives you:** every room shows "3 of 5 done" and the whole sheet shows an overall percentage. Finished rooms are marked Complete. A client or supervisor can see where the project stands in a few seconds.

### 5.2 Finish Schedule

- **In plain words:** a grid with rooms down the side and surfaces (floor, wall, ceiling and any you add) across the top. Each box holds a material and a code.
- **The problem it solves:** the carpenter, the painter and the tile supplier each work from a different understanding of what goes where.
- **When you would use it:** you want one reference sheet that every supplier and worker can use, saying "master bedroom: floor is oak laminate, code F2".
- **How it is used:**
  1. Add the rooms and surfaces.
  2. Fill each box with the material and its code.
- **What it gives you:** a single reference for what goes where, listing where each code is used. **If the same code has been given to two different materials, it warns you**, which catches the kind of mistake that normally shows up on site.

### 5.3 Furniture Spec Card

- **In plain words:** a card for one piece of furniture with a photo, name, size, material, finish, quantity and supplier.
- **The problem it solves:** a carpenter is given a verbal description and builds something a few inches off. Or three people ask the same question about the same piece.
- **When you would use it:** a custom wardrobe is being made. You want the carpenter to have the size, the finish, the quantity and the picture on one card.
- **How it is used:** fill in the details and add a photo. Sizes can be in mm, cm, inches or feet.
- **What it gives you:** a complete, unambiguous brief. All the details sit with the picture, so the same question is not asked three times. Quantity is always a whole number.

### 5.4 Moodboard

- **In plain words:** a free board where you place pictures and colour swatches, add captions and ask the client to approve.
- **The problem it solves:** the client says "I'll know it when I see it", and the look is only agreed after money has been spent.
- **When you would use it:** before ordering anything for a living room, you place sofa, fabric, paint and lighting references together and ask the client to approve the look.
- **How it is used:**
  1. Add pictures and swatches and drag them where you want them. They cannot be dragged off the board.
  2. Add captions.
  3. Choose who approves (see Part B).
- **What it gives you:**
  - Agreement on the look before spending.
  - Every approval stamped with name and time.
  - **Once approved, the board is locked** so it cannot drift. It can be reopened, and the earlier approval is kept on record.

### 5.5 Site Measurement Sheet

- **In plain words:** notes from measuring a room: each wall's length and height, the doors and windows, and a note with an optional photo of your hand sketch.
- **The problem it solves:** measurements are written on scraps of paper, and the wall area is worked out later, by hand, with mistakes.
- **When you would use it:** you visit a room to measure for paint and panelling. You want the areas worked out as you measure.
- **How it is used:**
  1. Enter each wall's length and height.
  2. Add the doors and windows.
  3. Add a note or a photo of your sketch.
- **What it gives you:** the **total wall length, the wall area, the area taken up by openings, and the net wall area**, worked out for you.
- **Good to know:**
  - A wall with no height uses the ceiling height.
  - Anything it cannot work out is listed as "not counted", instead of being guessed.
  - It warns you if the openings look larger than the walls.

## 6. Construction (6 elements)

**The idea in one line:** the day-by-day records a site is asked to show.

These are the **records that fill up day after day**, with a row for every defect, check, cube or item. They sit alongside the Site Work Order, RFI, Inspection Request and Daily Site Log you already have. Those are *forms you raise and send*. These are *working sheets* where the lines get filled in on site.

### 6.1 Snag / Punch List

- **In plain words:** a numbered list of defects found in a building or room. Each has a location, a description, an owner, a due date and an optional photo.
- **The problem it solves:** at handover, defects are noted on paper or in messages. Some are "fixed" but never checked, and some are forgotten, so the handover drags on.
- **When you would use it:** before handing over a flat, the PM walks through and records every small defect: a chipped tile, a door that does not close, a missing socket cover.
- **How it is used:**
  1. Add each defect with its location, description, owner and due date. Attach a photo if useful.
  2. The owner fixes it and marks it **Fixed**.
  3. A named person checks it and marks it **Verified**.
- **What it gives you:**
  - A snag is only closed when someone named has checked it.
  - Overdue snags show first, in red.
  - A summary such as "12 snags · 7 verified · 3 fixed, awaiting check · 2 open · 1 overdue", with the percentage cleared.
  - **Snag numbers never change.** If snag 4 is deleted, snag 5 stays snag 5.

### 6.2 Inspection Checklist

- **In plain words:** a list of checks for one stage of work (pre-pour, reinforcement, waterproofing and so on). Each line is marked Pass, Fail or N/A, with a remark.
- **The problem it solves:** the Inspection Request asks for an inspection to happen, but where is the record of what was found? Often it is a verbal "looks fine".
- **When you would use it:** before concrete is poured, the engineer checks the reinforcement against a list: cover, spacing, binding, cleanliness.
- **How it is used:**
  1. Choose the stage and the usual checks load for you. Edit, remove or add your own.
  2. Mark each line Pass, Fail or N/A, with a remark.
  3. Send it for approval (see Part B). A rejection needs a reason.
- **What it gives you:**
  - **It can only be accepted when every line has passed (or is N/A).** A pending or failed line blocks it and tells you how many.
  - Each approval stamp is visible to everyone.
  - While it is with an approver, and once approved, the lines are locked until it is reopened.
- **Good to know:** the Inspection Request *asks* for an inspection. This checklist *records the result*. They work as a pair.

### 6.3 Concrete Pour Card

- **In plain words:** the details of one pour (where, which member, grade, volume, slump) and the cube test results.
- **The problem it solves:** cube strength results come back days later and end up in a different file from the pour they belong to.
- **When you would use it:** a column is poured with M25 concrete. Cubes are cast, and tested at 7 and 28 days. You want the results kept with that pour.
- **How it is used:**
  1. Enter the pour details and the grade (M25, for example).
  2. Add each cube and its test result.
- **What it gives you:**
  - Each cube marked **Meets**, **Below** or **Not tested**, with the average for 7 and 28 days.
  - **When each test is due**, worked out from the pour date, and a note when a result is overdue.
- **Good to know:** the check is **indicative**, against the grade. Final acceptance follows your project specification.

### 6.4 Measurement Sheet

- **In plain words:** the measurement book as a sheet: rows of number × length × breadth × depth, with deductions.
- **The problem it solves:** quantities for a bill must be defensible. A total on a calculator with no workings cannot be checked.
- **When you would use it:** you measure the excavation for a foundation, with deductions for a pit that is not excavated, before preparing a bill.
- **How it is used:**
  1. Add a row for each measurement (number, length, breadth, depth).
  2. Put deductions on their own rows.
- **What it gives you:**
  - Additions, deductions and the **net quantity**.
  - A blank length, breadth or depth is simply left out, as in a measurement book. Deductions are on their own rows, so there are no negative numbers to get wrong.
  - A row with a number it cannot use is flagged and left out, rather than guessed.
  - A gentle hint appears if the dimensions do not suit the unit (for example, m³ with only two dimensions).

### 6.5 Running Bill Tracker

- **In plain words:** a running account bill: items with the BOQ quantity, rate, previous quantity and this bill's quantity.
- **The problem it solves:** with each running bill, it is easy to lose track of how much of each item has already been billed, and to bill more than the contract quantity.
- **When you would use it:** a contractor sends the third running bill. You want to see how much each item has progressed and what is payable now.
- **How it is used:** for each item, enter the BOQ quantity, the rate, the previous quantity and this bill's quantity.
- **What it gives you:**
  - For every item: the quantity to date, the percentage complete, and the amount for this bill.
  - **An item above its BOQ quantity is flagged** in amber.
  - Gross, GST, retention, advance recovery and the **net payable**. A fold-out explains the sum in plain words.
- **Good to know:** it is a simplified view. Check it against your contract terms.

### 6.6 Permit to Work

- **In plain words:** a permit for risky work (hot work, working at height, excavation, confined space, electrical isolation, lifting), with its precautions, validity window, approval and close-out.
- **The problem it solves:** risky work starts without the safety steps being confirmed, and nobody accountable has agreed to it. When something goes wrong, there is no record.
- **When you would use it:** a welder needs to work near stored materials. The permit lists the precautions (fire extinguisher present, area cleared, fire watch assigned) and must be approved before the work starts.
- **How it is used:**
  1. Choose the type of risky work and answer each precaution.
  2. Set the time window, and choose who approves (see Part B).
  3. Once approved, work can start within the validity window.
  4. At the end, close the permit with a name and a tick that the area has been made safe.
- **What it gives you:**
  - **Issued by** fills itself in with the person who dropped the element. Nobody types it, so it cannot be changed.
  - It can only be sent when every precaution is answered and no "No" is left, and the end time is still ahead.
  - Each approver's name and the date and time are stamped on the permit and visible to everyone.
  - Once approved it shows **Valid**, **Expires within the hour**, or **Expired: stop work and close**. Cancelling needs a reason.

## Part D. Good to know (limits and honest notes)

These are worth telling your management team up front, so nobody is surprised.

- **All rates and limits can be changed.** Calculator rates, warning periods (for example "warn 30 days before expiry") and yield targets are sensible starting points, not rules. Set them to match your business.
- **Approvals are a clear working record, not a legal e-signature.** They are stamped with the approver's name and the time, taken from their signed-in account.
- **Food and site records are working records, not certified systems.** They are a good, tidy record, but they do not replace any audit, statutory permit format or code-based acceptance your regulator or contract requires.
- **Uploads need a subtask.** Photos, artwork, snag photos and sketches are saved inside a subtask, so open one before uploading.
- **View-only people can see everything but change nothing.** They cannot sign, approve, tick, log or upload.
- **If two people edit the same element at the same moment,** the most recent save wins. It is best to let one person update a record at a time.
- **Calculators are estimates.** They are right when the inputs are right. Check the figures against your own experience before sending a quote.

## Part E. Where to start

If you only introduce a few elements, these give the most value quickly.

| Start with | Because | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Proof Markup** and **Artwork Approval Card** | Stops printing mistakes and "I never approved that" disputes | Printing and signage vendors |
| **Signature Pad** and **Photo Grid** | Proof of work and sign-off in one place | Everyone |
| **GST & Margin Calculator** | Prices a job correctly in a minute | Anyone who quotes |
| **Expiry & Shelf-Life Tracker** and **Licence Tracker** | Nothing lapses unnoticed | Food and FMCG |
| **Site Measurement Sheet** and **Moodboard** | Measure once, and get the look approved before work starts | Interior designers |
| **Snag List**, **Inspection Checklist** and **Permit to Work** | The records a site is asked to show, in one place | Contractors and site teams |

### How to roll it out

1. **Pick one group** that matches your biggest trade, not all six at once.
2. **Run one real project** with two or three elements from that group.
3. **Review after two weeks:** what saved time, what was confusing, what was missing.
4. **Then widen** to the next group.

## Part F. Quick answers for questions management may ask

**"What does this save us?"**
Rework, reprints, disputes and lost time. The same few mistakes (wrong price, unclear feedback, unapproved change, expired licence, unchecked defect) are the ones this is built to stop.

**"Can the vendor go straight to the client with an approval?"**
No. A vendor asks the PM (or a CAS member), and the PM decides what goes to the client. This is enforced by the system.

**"Can an approval be changed afterwards?"**
No. Once approved, the element is locked. It can be reopened for a correction, and the earlier decision stays on record.

**"Is the approval legally binding?"**
It is a clear, time-stamped working record. It is not a legal e-signature.

**"Do we have to use all 37?"**
No. They are independent. Use only the ones that match your work.

**"What if our rates are different from the example ones?"**
Change them. Every starting rate and limit can be set to your own.

**"Who can see these elements?"**
Everyone on the canvas can see them. People with view-only access can see everything and change nothing.
